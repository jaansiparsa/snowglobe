Title, Summary & Team Members

We will build Snow Globe of Anything, a real-time, interactive snow globe in the browser that lets you place any subject — a photo, a hand-drawn sketch, or a 3D Gaussian-splat capture — inside a glass dome and shake it to stir up the snow. The project centers on three classic graphics problems working together in real time: physically-based refraction through curved glass and water, particle-based snow dynamics, and swirling fluid motion of the water. The result is a shareable toy that doubles as a focused study of transmission rendering and coupled particle–fluid simulation.

Problem Description

A snow globe is a familiar object, but recreating one convincingly in real time touches several core computer-graphics problems at once. Light bends twice on its way to the eye — through the curved glass dome and again across the water surface — so the subject inside appears shifted, magnified, and tinted. Thousands of snow flakes must float, settle, and then churn back up when the globe is disturbed. And the water itself should swirl with momentum rather than snapping to rest.

The problem we are solving is to render and simulate all three effects — refraction, snow, and swirling water — together, interactively, in a web browser, while allowing the enclosed subject to be an arbitrary representation (an image, a sketch, or a captured Gaussian splat). This is important because it is a compact setting in which transmission rendering and coupled particle–fluid motion must coexist within a strict real-time frame budget, and it is challenging because: (a) refraction through a curved dome and a water interface means tracking multiple index-of-refraction boundaries and Fresnel reflection; (b) snow particles must be driven by a time-varying velocity field so they respond to shaking; and (c) embedding an arbitrary subject inside a refractive medium requires blending different scene representations convincingly.

Our general approach: a WebGL2/Three.js renderer using screen-space / environment-mapped refraction with Fresnel reflections for the glass, a GPU particle system for the snow, and a curl-noise (and, as a stretch goal, true fluid-solver) velocity field for the swirling water. Shaking is driven by mouse drag on desktop and the device-orientation / motion API on a phone, injecting angular velocity into the water field.

Goals & Deliverables

This is the core of the proposal. We describe what we expect to produce, how we will measure quality, and we split the work into a baseline we are confident we can ship and an aspirational plan if things go well.

What we will measure

  • Performance: interactive frame rate (target ≥ 60 fps on a laptop GPU) as a function of snow-particle count — reported as a particle-count-vs-fps scaling graph.
  • Visual quality: side-by-side comparison of our real-time refraction against an offline ray-traced reference of the same scene.
  • Interaction: latency and responsiveness of the shake-to-swirl coupling.

Questions we plan to answer

  • How convincing can screen-space / environment-mapped refraction be compared to a ray-traced ground truth, inside a real-time budget?
  • How many snow particles can we simulate and draw while staying interactive?
  • Can a Gaussian-splat subject be placed inside a refractive medium and still look believable?

PLAN What we plan to deliver

Our baseline for a successful project, with room for unexpected problems.

  • An interactive glass snow globe with refraction through the dome and Fresnel reflections.
  • A GPU snow particle system (thousands of flakes) that settles under gravity and is kicked up on shake.
  • Swirling water motion via a curl-noise velocity field coupled to the snow particles.
  • Shake interaction by mouse drag (desktop) and device motion (phone).
  • At least one subject type inside — a photo or sketch mapped onto a plane/billboard.
  • A performance study (particle-count vs. fps) and a refraction quality comparison.

HOPE What we hope to deliver

Aspirational goals if the baseline comes together early.

  • Gaussian-splat subject import, so you can drop a real 3D capture of your friends inside.
  • Caustics and chromatic dispersion through the glass for richer light transport.
  • A real fluid solver (2D/3D Stable Fluids or SPH) replacing the procedural noise field.
  • Snow accumulation on the base and collisions with the enclosed subject.
  • Shareable snapshots / permalinks of a configured globe.

Schedule

Presentations are about four weeks after the proposal deadline, so we assign a concrete set of tasks to each week and split work across the four team members.

Week 1Scene & glass
  • Set up the Three.js/WebGL2 project, build the dome geometry, base, and camera.
  • First-pass glass refraction shader; load and position a subject image inside.
  • Agree on the data flow between rendering and simulation modules.
Week 2Snow & input
  • GPU particle system: spawn, gravity, and settling behavior for snow.
  • Shake input via mouse drag and the device-orientation/motion API.
  • Basic swirling via a curl-noise velocity field driving the particles.
Week 3Coupling & polish
  • Improve glass: Fresnel, environment reflections, and the water-surface interface.
  • Tune the particle–fluid coupling so shaking convincingly stirs the snow.
  • Instrument and collect performance measurements; produce the scaling graph.
Week 4Stretch & write-up
  • Pursue stretch goals (splat import and/or caustics) as time allows.
  • Final polish, record the demo video, and run the ray-traced quality comparison.
  • Write the final report and prepare the presentation.

Resources

References

  • Pharr, Jakob & Humphreys, Physically Based Rendering: From Theory to Implementation — refraction, Fresnel, and transmission. pbr-book.org
  • Bridson, Houriham & Nordenstam, “Curl-Noise for Procedural Fluid Flow,” SIGGRAPH 2007 — divergence-free velocity fields for swirling motion.
  • Stam, “Stable Fluids,” SIGGRAPH 1999 — real-time fluid solver for the stretch goal.
  • Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler & Drettakis, “3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering,” SIGGRAPH 2023. project page
  • Three.js documentation — MeshPhysicalMaterial transmission, GPU particles, and environment maps. threejs.org/docs

Platform, hardware & software

  • Platform: the web — WebGL2 via Three.js, with GLSL shaders; runs in desktop and mobile browsers.
  • Hardware: personal laptops (macOS) for development and a phone for the device-motion shake interaction.
  • Software: JavaScript/TypeScript, Three.js, Vite for the dev build, and Git/GitHub for collaboration.

Starting point & existing code

We start from the open-source Three.js library and its example transmission-material and GPU-particle demos, which we will adapt rather than use as-is. Our own work lives in the project repository: github.com/jaansiparsa/snowglobe.
