01 Title, Summary & Team

A real-time snow globe you can put anything inside

We will build Snow Globe of Anything, an interactive snow globe in the browser that lets you place any subject — a photo, a hand-drawn sketch, or a 3D Gaussian-splat capture — inside a glass dome and shake it to stir up the snow. The project centers on three classic graphics problems working together in real time: physically-based refraction through curved glass and water, particle-based snow dynamics, and the swirling fluid motion of the water. The result is a shareable toy that doubles as a focused study of transmission rendering and coupled particle–fluid simulation.

02 Problem Description

Three hard effects, together, in real time

A snow globe is a familiar object, but recreating one convincingly in real time touches several core graphics problems at once. Light bends twice on its way to the eye — through the curved glass dome and again across the water surface — so the subject inside appears shifted, magnified, and tinted. Thousands of snow flakes must float, settle, and then churn back up when the globe is disturbed. And the water itself should swirl with momentum rather than snapping to rest.

The problem we are solving is to render and simulate all three effects — refraction, snow, and swirling water — together, interactively, in a browser, while allowing the enclosed subject to be an arbitrary representation. It is challenging because refraction through a curved dome and a water interface means tracking multiple index-of-refraction boundaries and Fresnel reflection; snow particles must be driven by a time-varying velocity field so they respond to shaking; and embedding an arbitrary subject inside a refractive medium requires blending different scene representations convincingly.

Our general approach is a WebGL2 / Three.js renderer using screen-space and environment-mapped refraction with Fresnel reflections for the glass, a GPU particle system for the snow, and a curl-noise velocity field — a true fluid solver as a stretch goal — for the swirling water. Shaking is driven by mouse drag on desktop and the device-motion API on a phone, injecting angular velocity into the water field.

03 Goals & Deliverables

What we will measure, and what we will ship

This is the core of the proposal. We split the work into a baseline we are confident we can deliver and an aspirational plan if things go well.

How we measure quality

  • Performance — interactive frame rate (target ≥ 60 fps on a laptop GPU) reported as a particle-count-vs-fps scaling graph.
  • Visual quality — side-by-side comparison of our real-time refraction against an offline ray-traced reference of the same scene.
  • Interaction — responsiveness of the shake-to-swirl coupling.

Questions we plan to answer

  • How convincing can screen-space refraction be versus a ray-traced ground truth, inside a real-time budget?
  • How many snow particles can we simulate and draw while staying interactive?
  • Can a Gaussian-splat subject sit inside a refractive medium and still look believable?

i. What we plan to deliver

Baseline for a successful project, with room for surprises.

  • An interactive glass snow globe with dome refraction and Fresnel reflections.
  • A GPU snow particle system (thousands of flakes) that settles under gravity and is kicked up on shake.
  • Swirling water via a curl-noise velocity field coupled to the snow.
  • Shake interaction by mouse drag and device motion.
  • At least one subject type — a photo or sketch on a plane.
  • A performance study and a refraction quality comparison.

ii. What we hope to deliver

Aspirational, if the baseline lands early.

  • Gaussian-splat subject import — drop a real 3D capture of your friends inside.
  • Caustics and chromatic dispersion through the glass.
  • A real fluid solver (Stable Fluids or SPH) replacing the procedural field.
  • Snow accumulation on the base and collisions with the subject.
  • Shareable snapshots and permalinks of a configured globe.

04 Schedule

Four weeks, a set of tasks each week

Presentations are about four weeks after the proposal deadline, so each week carries concrete tasks split across the four team members.

Week 1Scene & glass
  • Set up the Three.js / WebGL2 project — dome geometry, base, and camera.
  • First-pass glass refraction shader; load and place a subject image.
  • Agree on the data flow between rendering and simulation.
Week 2Snow & input
  • GPU particle system — spawn, gravity, and settling for the snow.
  • Shake input via mouse drag and the device-motion API.
  • Basic swirling via a curl-noise velocity field driving particles.
Week 3Coupling & polish
  • Improve glass — Fresnel, environment reflections, water interface.
  • Tune the particle–fluid coupling so shaking convincingly stirs the snow.
  • Instrument performance; produce the scaling graph.
Week 4Stretch & write-up
  • Pursue stretch goals — splat import and/or caustics — as time allows.
  • Final polish, demo video, and the ray-traced quality comparison.
  • Write the final report and prepare the presentation.

05 Resources

References, platform & starting point

References

  • Pharr, Jakob & Humphreys, Physically Based Rendering — refraction, Fresnel, transmission. pbr-book.org
  • Bridson et al., “Curl-Noise for Procedural Fluid Flow,” SIGGRAPH 2007 — divergence-free swirling fields.
  • Stam, “Stable Fluids,” SIGGRAPH 1999 — real-time fluid solver for the stretch goal.
  • Kerbl et al., “3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering,” SIGGRAPH 2023. project page
  • Three.js documentation — transmission material, GPU particles, environment maps. threejs.org/docs

Platform, hardware & software

  • Platform — the web: WebGL2 via Three.js with GLSL shaders; runs in desktop and mobile browsers.
  • Hardware — personal laptops (macOS) for development and a phone for the device-motion shake.
  • Software — JavaScript / TypeScript, Three.js, Vite, and Git / GitHub.

Starting point

We start from the open-source Three.js library and its transmission-material and GPU-particle examples, adapting rather than using them as-is. Our own work lives in the project repository: github.com/jaansiparsa/snowglobe.
